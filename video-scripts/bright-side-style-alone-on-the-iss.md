# Bright Side: Channel Breakdown + Original Script

## Part 1: How the channel works

**Scale:** about 44.6M subscribers, about 11.9B views and more than 11,600 uploads (third-party stats, July 2026). Bright Side belongs to the same company as 5-Minute Crafts. It runs like a content factory: daily uploads, one house style and a team of anonymous writers.

### Content pillars (what they cover)
| Pillar | Example angle | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Space & science "discoveries"** | "Voyager 1 Found a Wall at the Edge of…" / "Scientists Just Found What…" | Awe + mystery; evergreen; easy to animate |
| **"What If" hypotheticals** | What if you fell into a black hole / the Moon disappeared / you never slept | Viewer is the protagonist; story structure built in |
| **Your body** | "Never sleep on your side: 5 reasons why" | Personal stakes ("this is happening to YOU") |
| **Survival** | "The only way to survive a free-falling elevator" | Fear + practical payoff |
| **Places no one can go** | "Nobody Will EVER Visit This Place" | Forbidden knowledge, curiosity gap |
| **True stories / history oddities** | People who survived impossible situations | Narrative, emotion |
| **Riddles & tests** | "Only 1% can solve…" | Interactivity, comments, ego |

### Title formulas
1. **Superlative/absolute:** "Nobody Will EVER…", "The Only Way to…", "Never Do X"
2. **Fresh discovery:** "Scientists Just Found…", "NASA Finally Revealed…"
3. **Cut-off mystery:** "Voyager 1 Found a Wall At…". The title stops before the payoff.
4. **Second-person hypothetical:** "What If You…", "What Happens to Your Body When…"
5. **Listicle + reason:** "15 Reasons Why…", "7 Signs…"

Shared traits: present tense, mostly 6–10 words, often one CAPITALIZED word for emphasis, and the payoff is always withheld.

### Thumbnails
They use bright, saturated cartoon art (in their 2D style, not photos), one huge focal object, and a shocked cartoon face or tiny human for scale. They also add a red arrow or circle, 0–3 words of text, and a strong contrast such as a hot orange object against a dark blue background.

### Hook patterns (first 15 seconds)
- **Drop the viewer into it:** "You wake up and the station is silent…" Second person and present tense, with no intro and no logo.
- **Shocking fact first:** "Right now, 24 billion km from Earth, something is still talking to us."
- **Stakes + countdown:** "You have 90 seconds before…"
- **Open loop:** "…and what happens on day 40 is the part nobody expects." The video then holds this back until near the end.
- **No "Hi guys, welcome back."** The channel identity is the voice and art style.

### Script structure & retention mechanics
1. **Cold open hook (0:00–0:20):** a scenario plus one open loop.
2. **Escalation in beats:** a timeline ("Hour 1… Day 3… Week 2…"), a countdown or a numbered list. Each beat is about 45–90 s and ends with a mini-cliffhanger.
3. **Fact-sandwich:** a hard number, then a relatable comparison, then a joke or reaction ("That's like stacking 3 Eiffel Towers… on your toe.").
4. **Re-hooks every ~60 s:** "But that's not even the scariest part." / "And this is where it gets weird."
5. **Light humor and a recurring character:** the cartoon "you" reacts and fails, which breaks up the dense facts.
6. **Payoff the open loop near the end**, then a quick upbeat outro.
7. **Engagement close:** a question to the viewer ("Would you have stayed? Tell me in the comments."). The end screen points to the next video.

**Delivery:** one calm, warm, slightly playful narrator; short sentences; roughly 150–160 wpm; 8–12 minutes (around 1,300–1,800 words). They use simple vocabulary, so every fact can be understood by a 12-year-old.

## Part 2: Original script in the same style

**Title options**
- **What If You Were Left Alone on the Space Station?** (primary)
- You're the LAST Person on the ISS. Here's How Long You'd Last
- Everyone Left the Space Station… Except You

**Thumbnail:** a tiny cartoon astronaut pressed against the Cupola window with a horrified face. Earth glows huge behind them and a red arrow points at a departing capsule. Text: **"WAIT!"**

**Target length:** about 9:30 (about 1,450 words)

---

### [0:00] COLD OPEN

*VISUAL: Black screen. Soft beep. Fade in on the cartoon you, floating in a sleeping bag strapped to a wall.*

You wake up and something is wrong. It's too quiet.

No one is laughing in the next module, no Russian music is drifting from the service module, and no one is complaining about the coffee.

*VISUAL: You float down a corridor. Empty. Empty. Empty.*

You check the docking ports. The capsule that brought your crew here is… gone. A note is stuck to the wall: *"Emergency evacuation. You were asleep. Sorry!! Help is coming."*

You're now the only human being off planet Earth, 400 kilometers up, moving at 28,000 kilometers an hour.

How long could you survive up here? And what happens on day 30 is the part nobody warns you about.

*SFX: Title sting.*

---

### [0:35] HOUR 1: DON'T PANIC (PANIC A LITTLE)

*VISUAL: You spin in circles, arms flailing.*

First, the good news: the International Space Station is basically designed to keep people alive even when everything goes wrong. It's the size of a football field, it cost more than 100 billion dollars, and it has been continuously crewed since November 2000.

The bad news is that it's built to be run by a team. A normal crew is six or seven people, and now there's one: you.

*VISUAL: A cartoon checklist unrolls… and keeps unrolling off-screen.*

Your first job is to call Mission Control. Luckily, you're not really alone. Teams in Houston and Moscow watch the station 24 hours a day. A voice crackles through your headset: "Hey… so… about that."

They tell you a rescue mission is being prepared, but launches don't happen overnight. You'll need to hold on for weeks.

*VISUAL: Calendar pages flutter.* Okay. Weeks. You can do weeks… right?

---

### [1:40] DAY 1: THE FOOD MATH

*VISUAL: You open a storage bag. Hundreds of foil pouches float out like confetti.*

Let's start with food. The ISS doesn't get restocked every day. Cargo ships arrive every few months, so the station always keeps a buffer of supplies on board.

That buffer was planned for a full crew. Now it's all yours. Food won't be what kills you.

*VISUAL: You bite a tortilla. Crumbs drift toward an air vent.*

Pro tip: astronauts eat tortillas instead of bread, because bread crumbs float around and get into equipment, and into your eyes.

*VISUAL: A crumb hits your eye. "OW."*

But food isn't your real problem. Water is.

---

### [2:35] DAY 2: DRINKING YESTERDAY'S COFFEE

*VISUAL: A shiny machine labeled "WATER RECOVERY SYSTEM." A tiny drop travels through pipes.*

On the ISS, almost every drop of water is recycled: the moisture from your breath, your sweat, and yes, your pee. The station's water system recovers about 98% of it and turns it back into clean drinking water.

As astronauts like to say: *"Yesterday's coffee becomes tomorrow's coffee."*

*VISUAL: You stare at your water pouch. Long pause. You drink it anyway.*

But that system needs maintenance. Filters clog and pumps break, and normally a whole crew shares those repair jobs. Now every alarm on the station is your alarm.

And this is where it gets weird.

---

### [3:25] DAY 4: SIXTEEN SUNRISES

*VISUAL: Time-lapse through the Cupola: day, night, day, night, flicking past.*

The ISS circles Earth about every 90 minutes. That means you see roughly 16 sunrises and 16 sunsets every single day.

When you're with a crew, a strict schedule keeps your body clock in line. Alone, it's easy to let that slip.

*VISUAL: You "sleep" at random angles: upside down, sideways, tangled in a cable.*

Without a routine, your sleep breaks apart. You nap for an hour, wake up, and watch another sunrise. You lose track of what day it is. Lack of sleep makes you slower, more forgetful, and more likely to make mistakes.

And up here, mistakes are expensive.

So you do what astronauts actually do: you set alarms, keep the lights on a schedule, and follow Mission Control's timeline like it's the law.

---

### [4:20] WEEK 1: YOUR BODY STARTS DISAPPEARING

*VISUAL: An X-ray version of you. Bones fade slightly, muscles shrink like deflating balloons.*

But that's not even the scariest part.

In weightlessness, your body thinks you don't need your skeleton anymore. Without exercise, astronauts can lose roughly 1% of the bone mass in their hips and spine every month. That's about as much as an elderly person on Earth loses in a whole year.

Your muscles shrink too, especially in your legs, since you're not standing on them.

*VISUAL: You float past the treadmill, which has bungee cords attached.*

That's why astronauts exercise about two hours a day. They run on a treadmill while bungee cords strap them down, and they lift "weights" on a machine that uses vacuum cylinders.

*VISUAL: You strap into the treadmill. Huff. Puff. Mission Control: "Great job! 1 hour 58 minutes to go."*

Meanwhile, fluid that would normally sink to your legs floats up to your head. Your face gets puffy, your legs get skinny, and astronauts call this "puffy face, bird legs."

*VISUAL: Cartoon you, now with a balloon face and chicken legs, salutes.*

---

### [5:20] WEEK 2: THE ALARM

*SFX: Loud, rhythmic alarm. Red lights pulse.*

At 3 a.m. (or whatever time it is), an alarm goes off. The display reads:

**"ATMOSPHERE."**

Your heart drops. Here are the three alarms every astronaut trains for:
1. **Fire**: there's no "outside" to run to.
2. **Rapid depressurization**: a leak, maybe from a tiny piece of space junk.
3. **Toxic atmosphere**: something like an ammonia leak from the cooling system.

*VISUAL: Three big cartoon icons light up one by one.*

Every one of these is designed to be handled by a team: one person checks the pressure, another isolates modules, and a third talks to the ground.

You'll have to do all three jobs yourself.

*VISUAL: You float frantically between panels, a phone cord tangling around you.*

Luckily, this one is small. A pressure sensor is acting up. Mission Control talks you through it step by step, and the alarm goes quiet.

You float in silence for a long time.

---

### [6:20] DAY 30: THE PART NOBODY WARNS YOU ABOUT

*VISUAL: The music drops out. Wide shot: tiny you in the Cupola, Earth enormous below.*

Remember that thing about day 30? Here it is.

The biggest danger of being alone in space isn't bones, water, or even alarms. It's your mind.

Researchers who study isolation (in Antarctic stations, submarines, and long mock missions to Mars) keep seeing the same pattern. Somewhere around the middle of a long mission, morale often hits its lowest point. Some researchers call this the "third-quarter effect." The excitement is gone, and the end still feels far away.

*VISUAL: You talk to a sock puppet. The sock puppet looks concerned.*

And you don't have a crew to joke with or argue with. You don't even have anyone to steal your favorite snack.

But astronauts report something else too, and it might save you.

*VISUAL: Slow push in on Earth. Thin blue atmosphere. City lights.*

Many astronauts who look back at Earth describe a powerful shift in perspective. They see a tiny, fragile planet with no visible borders, and every person they've ever loved is down there. It's called the **Overview Effect**.

So you make a routine. You call your family every day, take photos of your hometown when you fly over it, and start a video diary.

You're not lonely. You're the person keeping the lights on for everybody down there.

---

### [7:40] DAY 45: KNOCK KNOCK

*VISUAL: A bright dot in the window grows larger. Thrusters puff.*

*SFX: Radio crackle.* "Station, this is the rescue capsule. We're 200 meters out."

Modern capsules can dock almost by themselves. Their sensors line them up with the docking port down to a few centimeters.

*SFX: Clunk. Hiss. A hatch opens.*

A face appears. "Hey. Heard you've been holding down the fort."

You open your mouth to say something cool and heroic…

*VISUAL: You burst into tears and hug them so hard you both spin into a wall.*

---

### [8:30] BACK ON EARTH

*VISUAL: A capsule under parachutes splashes into the ocean.*

After a long stay in space, astronauts usually can't walk normally right away. Their balance system has to relearn which way is "down." Some describe feeling like everything is too heavy, even a pen.

*VISUAL: You try to drop your phone into the air expecting it to float. It hits the floor. You stare at it.*

Full recovery of bone and muscle can take months. But you made it: 45 days, alone, 400 kilometers above everyone you know.

---

### [9:05] OUTRO

*VISUAL: Cartoon you in a chair at home, staring up at the night sky. A tiny dot moves across it.*

Here's something cool: you can see the ISS with your own eyes. It looks like a bright star moving steadily across the sky, with no blinking. NASA's "Spot the Station" website will tell you when it passes over your home.

So, would you have stayed calm up there, or would you have been talking to a sock by day three? Tell me in the comments!

*VISUAL: End screen, with two video tiles: "What If You Fell Into a Black Hole?" and "Nobody Will EVER Visit This Place."*

## Production notes
- **Word count:** about 1,450 spoken words, so 9–10 min at 150 wpm.
- **Re-hooks:** about every 60 s ("And this is where it gets weird," "But that's not even the scariest part," and the payoff of the "Day 30" open loop).
- **Fact check before recording:** the facts here (400 km altitude, ~28,000 km/h, crewed since Nov 2000, ~98% water recovery, ~16 sunrises/day, ~1%/month bone loss, ~2 h/day exercise, third-quarter effect, Overview Effect, Spot the Station) are widely reported. Still, check the exact numbers against NASA sources and state any approximations as approximations.
- **Fiction disclaimer:** the evacuation scenario is made up. In reality, crews always keep a docked lifeboat capsule with a seat for every crew member.
